The AM–GM inequality

For nonnegative numbers, the arithmetic mean is always at least as large as the geometric mean. This proof builds the result using two moves: doubling the number of terms, and stepping down by one.

The statement

Arithmetic mean ≥ geometric mean

For a positive integer \(n\) and nonnegative real numbers \(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\), \[ \frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n}{n}\;\geq\;\sqrt[n]{x_1x_2\cdots x_n}. \]

Base case

Check \(n=1\) and \(n=2\)

For \(n=1\), both sides equal \(x_1\). For \(n=2\), the square \((\sqrt{x_1}-\sqrt{x_2})^2\) is nonnegative, so \[ x_1+x_2\geq 2\sqrt{x_1x_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{x_1+x_2}{2}\geq\sqrt{x_1x_2}. \]

Doubling

If it holds for \(k\), it holds for \(2k\)

Assume AM–GM holds for \(k\) nonnegative numbers. Apply it to each half of a list of \(2k\) numbers, and let \[ A=\sqrt[k]{x_1x_2\cdots x_k},\qquad B=\sqrt[k]{x_{k+1}x_{k+2}\cdots x_{2k}}. \] The two applications give \[ \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_{2k}}{2k} \geq \frac{A+B}{2}. \] The already-proved case \(n=2\), applied to \(A\) and \(B\), says \(\frac{A+B}{2}\geq\sqrt{AB}\). Therefore, \[ \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_{2k}}{2k} \geq \sqrt{AB} = \sqrt[2k]{x_1x_2\cdots x_{2k}}. \] Thus the result for \(k\) implies the result for \(2k\).

Step down

If it holds for \(l\), it holds for \(l-1\)

Assume AM–GM holds for \(l\geq2\), and choose \[ x_l=\frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_{l-1}}{l-1}. \] This choice makes the average of all \(l\) numbers equal to \(x_l\). The assumed inequality gives \[ x_l\geq\sqrt[l]{x_1x_2\cdots x_{l-1}x_l}. \] If \(x_l=0\), all of \(x_1,\dots,x_{l-1}\) are zero, and AM–GM for those numbers holds with equality. If \(x_l>0\), raise both sides to the \(l\)-th power and divide by \(x_l\): \[ x_l^{l-1}\geq x_1x_2\cdots x_{l-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_{l-1}}{l-1} \geq\sqrt[l-1]{x_1x_2\cdots x_{l-1}}. \] So the result for \(l\) implies the result for \(l-1\).

✓

Why this proves every case

Starting from \(n=1\) and \(n=2\), the doubling step proves the result for every power of two. To prove it for any chosen positive integer \(n\), take a power of two at least as large as \(n\), then repeatedly use the step-down result until reaching \(n\). Therefore AM–GM holds for every positive integer by Cauchy induction.
